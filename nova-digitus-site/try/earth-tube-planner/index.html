    Design A

    Earth-tube build spec for an Olive Branch yard

    The video build is in dry country. Mississippi soil and air change three things: the ground runs warmer, summer dewpoints sit around 70–74°F, and clay holds water. This spec adjusts for that.

    1. Call 811 and check the water table

      Mississippi 811 locates lines free. Dig a test hole to full depth after a rain. If water seeps in above your pipe depth, go shallower or pick another spot; a flooded tube is a mold tube.

    2. Dig 10–12 ft if you can, 8 ft minimum

      At 4–6 ft the August soil here is 72°F and up. Every extra foot cuts the summer swing. A mini-excavator rental gets you there in a day.

    3. Several shorter 6-in runs, not one long 4-in

      4 to 6 parallel runs of 100–150 ft on a manifold, spaced at least 3 ft apart so each has its own cool soil. Bigger pipe drops air speed, fan power and noise.

    4. Schedule 40, solvent-welded, bedded in sand

      Your lesson holds: Sch 40 only. Lay 4–6 in of sand or pea gravel, set the pipe, then hand-place the first foot of backfill with no rocks before the machine touches it.

    5. Slope 1–2% to a sealed condensate sump

      Clay will not drain into pea gravel the way desert soil does. Run the low end to a sealed sump with a trap and a small condensate pump. An open drain in clay lets groundwater and soil gas into the pipe.

    6. Run it as a closed loop in summer

      Pull air from the house, through the ground, back into the house. Indoor air at 50% RH has a dewpoint near 56°F, so it stays dry in the pipe. Use outdoor-air mode in spring and fall when dewpoints are low.

    7. Filter the intake and keep a dehumidifier

      MERV 8 filter at the intake, screened cap, at least 3 ft above grade and away from the AC condenser. A dehumidifier handles the latent load the tube cannot. Run a radon test after install.

    Alternate design

    Design B: water-loop ground coil

    Move water through the ground instead of air. A small pump circulates water through coils of 1-in HDPE pipe buried in a trench. The cooled water feeds a fan coil inside the house, like a car radiator running in reverse. Water carries heat far better than air, so the pipe is small and cheap, and no air you breathe ever touches the soil. Condensation forms on the indoor coil and drains from a pan, the same as a regular AC.
